Chapter One

The Villa Rose stood a little apart from the road, half hidden by old cedars, its pale walls taking the last of the evening light. Madame Lauzun had kept the tables running since sundown, and the long room behind the shuttered windows had filled slowly with the kind of company that gambles quietly and loses gracefully.

Ricardo came late, as he always did, and stood for a moment at the edge of the light, letting his eyes go around the table. He had a young Englishman with him that evening, a Mr. Wethermill, lately down from Oxford, with that agreeable confidence which money and a good manner lend to a man of eight and twenty.

He was a little dashed, this young man, by the stillness of the room. The deal went round without a word above the murmur of the stakes, and the croupier's rake swept in the counters from either side with a motion as regular as the swing of a pendulum.

"They play high here," Ricardo said, under his breath.

"So much the better," Wethermill answered. "I did not come to France to play for coppers."

At the far end of the table a woman in black sat with an indifferent look of boredom on her cleanly chiselled face. It was plain that her good fortune stayed at home tonight, for opposite to her the croupier was arranging with extraordinary deftness the piles of bank-notes in the order of their value.

The bank was winning heavily. Even as Ricardo looked, Wethermill turned up a natural, and the croupier swept in the stakes from either side with a smile that did not reach his eyes.

Chapter Two

"Fautes vos jeux, messieurs. Le jeu est fait." The croupier cried it all in a breath, and repeated the words with the wooden patience of a man who has said the same thing five thousand times in a night and will say it five thousand more.

Wethermill waited with his hand upon the wooden frame in which the cards were stacked. He glanced round the table while the stakes were being laid upon the cloth, and suddenly his face flashed from languor into interest.

Almost opposite to him a note was thrust forward by a gloved hand holding a five-louis chip between two fingers. The hand was firm, the fingers white at the tips from the pressure of the rings, and the arm that carried it was bare to the shoulder and very beautiful.

Wethermill leaned forward and shook his head with a smile. With a gesture he refused the stake. The woman in black did not move a muscle; but her eyes came up for the first time that night and rested upon his face for the space of a second.

"It is Mlle. of Chambery," Ricardo whispered. "She has lost everything she brought, and now she stakes what she has not. You did wisely to refuse her."

"There is no wisdom in it," Wethermill answered lightly. "I refused her because I did not wish to win from her. One does not take bread out of a pretty hand."

Beyond the shrubbery a motor car passed along the high road without stopping, its lamps sweeping for an instant across the shuttered windows of the villa. The play went on. The bank won, and won again, and the young Englishman's luck held like a rope of sand holds water.

Chapter Three

At two in the morning the tables closed, and the company straggled out into the cold of the garden, where the cicadas had given up and only the pines talked overhead. Wethermill stood a moment on the gravel, breathing the smell of the pines, and wondered why the woman in black had looked at him so.

Ricardo found him there, smoking in the dark. "You are thinking of the note she staked," he said. "You should not. That woman is a mystery, and mysteries in this country are like the mists on the marshes. They look romantic at a distance and they chill you to the bone when you walk into them."

"I was not thinking of her," Wethermill said. "I was thinking that I have won two hundred louis and that tomorrow I shall go back to Paris. There is nothing in this province but heat and cards."

"There is the Villa Rose," said Ricardo quietly.

"And the Villa Rose is a shelter for gamblers and their money. Good night to it."

Yet he looked back, as men do, when the road turned. The shuttered windows stood grey against the stars, and somewhere behind them the croupier was counting the tallies of the night. A light still burned in one upper room, small and steady, like the light of a candle carried through a long corridor.

It was three days later, when the newspapers told of a theft at the villa and of a woman who had disappeared with a bank's weak hours behind her, that Wethermill remembered that light, and understood at last that he had seen the beginning of the story which all Paris would be talking about before the week was out.
